Few buyers pay cash for a business. Most Canadian acquisitions combine several sources of money, and a well-built financing plan is often what convinces a seller to choose you. Here are the main options.

## Your equity

Lenders want to see that you have "skin in the game". Expect to contribute a meaningful share of the purchase price from your own funds, often 15% to 30% or more depending on the lender, the business and your experience. Newcomers should also plan for **working capital** after closing (payroll, inventory, rent) and for the costs of advisors, licences and settling in.

## Bank and credit union loans

Canada's chartered banks and credit unions finance business acquisitions, usually secured against the business's assets and backed by a personal guarantee. They will look closely at:

- historical cash flow and how comfortably it covers the loan payments,
- your management experience in the industry,
- the quality of the financial statements, and
- any real estate or equipment that can serve as collateral.

## BDC

The **Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC)** is a federal Crown corporation that offers loans specifically for buying or transferring a business, including share purchases. BDC often takes on financing that conventional lenders won't, and it can work alongside a bank or a vendor take-back.

## The Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP)

Under the **CSBFP**, the federal government shares the risk on loans that participating banks and credit unions make to small businesses. In 2026 a borrower can access up to **$1.15 million** in total:

- up to **$1 million** in term loans, of which no more than $500,000 can go to equipment and leasehold improvements (and of that, up to $150,000 for intangible assets and working capital), and
- up to **$150,000** in lines of credit.

The CSBFP can finance *eligible assets* of a business you're buying, such as equipment, leasehold improvements and real property. It **cannot** cover the whole purchase price (for example goodwill or a share premium), so it is usually combined with other financing.

## Vendor take-back (VTB)

With a **vendor take-back**, the seller lends you part of the purchase price, which you repay over time, typically from the business's cash flow. VTBs are common in Canadian small business sales because they:

- reduce the cash and bank debt you need at closing,
- keep the seller invested in a smooth handover, and
- signal that the seller believes the business will keep performing.

Terms are fully negotiable: amount, interest rate, repayment schedule, and whether payments are subordinated to the bank. With so many owners planning to retire and struggling to find buyers, sellers are often open to a VTB.

## Earn-outs

An **earn-out** ties part of the price to future performance, for example a payment if revenue hits a target in the first two years. Earn-outs can bridge a gap between what the seller asks and what you think the business is worth, but they need careful drafting.

## Putting it together: an illustrative structure

A hypothetical $800,000 purchase might be funded as:

| Source | Amount | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer equity | $200,000 | 25% |
| Bank / BDC term loan (including CSBFP-eligible assets) | $440,000 | 55% |
| Vendor take-back over 3–5 years | $160,000 | 20% |

*Illustrative only. Actual structures depend on the lender, the business and the buyer.*

## Tips for newcomers

- Build a relationship with a Canadian bank early, and open accounts as soon as you can.
- Gather proof of funds and their source, since lenders and immigration programs may both ask for it.
- Have a CPA prepare projections that show how the business will service the debt.
