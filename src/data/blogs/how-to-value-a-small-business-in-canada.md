"What is this business actually worth?" It is the most important question in any purchase. In CFIB's 2026 research, 37% of Canadian owners also said not knowing how to value their business was a barrier to selling. This guide explains the methods brokers, lenders and buyers use, so you can judge whether an asking price is reasonable.

## Start with normalized earnings

Valuation starts with what the business earns for its owner. Financial statements of owner-operated businesses usually need to be **normalized**: adjusted for items that won't continue after the sale. Examples:

- the owner's salary and personal expenses run through the company,
- one-time costs (a lawsuit, a major repair),
- family members paid above or below market rates,
- rent paid to a property the owner also controls.

## SDE: the small-business standard

For most owner-operated "main street" businesses, the key number is **seller's discretionary earnings (SDE)**: pre-tax earnings plus the owner's salary and benefits, interest, depreciation and amortization, and one-time or personal expenses.

SDE tells you how much cash flow is available to *one* full-time owner-operator, to pay themselves, service debt and reinvest.

## EBITDA for larger businesses

Once a business has a management team and the owner isn't essential day to day, buyers usually value it on **EBITDA**: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, *after* paying a market salary to whoever runs it. EBITDA multiples are typically higher than SDE multiples because the business doesn't depend on its owner.

## What multiples look like

Price is often expressed as a multiple of earnings. Large datasets of small-business listings in 2026 show most main-street businesses asking roughly **2× to 4× SDE**, with a median around 2.6× to 2.7×. Multiples rise with:

- **Size.** Businesses with higher earnings command higher multiples.
- **Independence from the owner.** Manager-run businesses sell for more than owner-operated ones.
- **Real estate.** When the property is included, the overall price (and multiple) is much higher.
- **Recurring revenue, diversified customers, growth and clean books.**

These figures come mostly from North American listing data. Canadian prices vary by province, sector and community, so treat them as a sanity check rather than a rule.

## Asset-based and market approaches

- **Asset-based valuation** sets a floor: what the equipment, inventory and property are worth on their own. It matters most for asset-heavy or underperforming businesses.
- **Comparable sales** look at what similar businesses actually sold for, which a broker or valuator can help you find.

## Red flags that should lower the price

- Revenue concentrated in one or two customers
- Declining sales over the last three years
- A lease ending soon with no right to renew
- Key relationships that sit only with the owner
- Financial statements that don't match tax returns

## Get a professional opinion

For a significant purchase, consider a **Chartered Business Valuator (CBV)** or an experienced CPA, especially if a lender or an immigration program will rely on the numbers. A well-supported valuation also gives you a stronger position at the negotiating table.
